A vaccine can save a life — but only if it reaches the child

Imagine a child arriving at a health facility for routine immunisation.

The vaccine exists. It may have been purchased, shipped to the country, and distributed through the national system.

But when the child arrives:

The vaccine is not there.

Perhaps the facility has run out of stock. Perhaps delivery was delayed. Perhaps the refrigerator failed. Perhaps poor records meant nobody realised supplies were running low.

For the child, the reason makes little difference.

Open with the child, not the analytical method. The purpose of the supply chain is to make vaccination possible at the point at which a child needs it.

Immunisation is also a supply-chain challenge

A vaccine can protect a child only if it is:

Available

At the right place, at the right time, in the right quantity.

Potent and safe

Stored and transported under appropriate conditions.

Reliably delivered

Supported by functioning people, equipment, information and transport.

Efficiently managed

Using limited resources effectively and sustainably.

A vaccine that does not reach the child cannot protect the child.

But what makes vaccines available?

There is no single action called “make vaccines available.”

Availability emerges from a system in which many things have to work together:

Forecasting & ordering → storage & cold chain → stock management → distribution → information → staff & supervision → continuous improvement

A weakness anywhere in this chain can eventually appear at the service point as the same outcome:

The vaccine the child needs is unavailable.

The decision problem

When vaccine availability is poor, where should a programme intervene?

Should it invest in cold-chain equipment? Improve stock management? Strengthen distribution? Train staff? Improve information systems? Change forecasting practices? Strengthen supervision?

Resources are limited.

Saying that “everything matters” is not enough.

We need evidence that helps identify where improvement efforts should be focused first.

EVM turns supply-chain performance into measurable evidence

Effective Vaccine Management (EVM) provides a systematic assessment of how well the immunisation supply chain is functioning.

Rather than asking only whether vaccines are available today, EVM examines the capabilities, processes and practices that should make reliable availability possible.

It covers areas such as:

Infrastructure and cold chain · Temperature management · Stock management · Distribution · Information · Maintenance · Management practices

EVM creates an analytical opportunity

EVM tells us where requirements are being met — and where weaknesses exist.

But a further question matters for decision-makers:

Which EVM requirements are associated with better vaccine availability?

This moves us from assessment toward evidence for prioritisation.

\[ \boxed{\text{EVM requirements} \longrightarrow \textbf{Vaccine availability}} \]

Our focus: Service Points

The analysis focuses on Service Point (SP) facilities — the places closest to where vaccines are ultimately delivered to people.

Outcome

Vaccine availability (AV): the proportion of assessed vaccines physically present at the facility.

Predictors of interest

The facility’s EVM requirements.

Context

Country and target population are used as adjustments rather than treated as the requirements of interest.

Availability is ordered, not simply “good” or “bad”

Availability is represented using six ordered bands:

Band Availability
1 — None AV = 0
2 — Critical 0 < AV ≤ 0.25
3 — Low 0.25 < AV ≤ 0.50
4 — Moderate 0.50 < AV ≤ 0.75
5 — Good 0.75 < AV < 1
6 — Full AV = 1

The distribution clusters at values such as 0, .25, .50, .75 and 1. The ordered representation respects ranking without assuming equal numeric distances are meaningful.

Preparing EVM requirements for analysis

A raw EVM value can contain two different messages:

How well is the requirement met?

A score represents the level of performance/compliance.

Does the requirement apply?

A not-applicable indicator captures a different structural situation.

These should not be confused.

A facility where a requirement does not apply is not necessarily equivalent to a facility that performs badly on that requirement.

The analysis therefore keeps score and applicability separate.

Why a simple comparison can mislead

Suppose facilities with stronger EVM scores also have better vaccine availability.

Is that because of the requirement itself?

Or because those facilities happen to be in countries with stronger health systems, infrastructure and funding?

Or because larger facilities are differently resourced?

We need to separate the requirement signal from country context and facility size.

We ask the question three ways

Model B — Main

Across all facilities, which requirements are linked to availability?

Adjusts for country context and target population.

Model A — Country check

When facilities are compared within their own country, which requirements still matter?

Removes the possibility that a requirement looks important simply because of its country.

Model C — Size check

What happens when facility size is not used?

Checks whether findings are driven by target population.

The strongest findings are those that survive all three views.

Finding signal among many requirements

There are too many correlated candidate requirements to fit safely in one ordinary model.

The analysis therefore uses two stages:

1. Find requirements that repeatedly matter

A penalised ordinal model (lasso) is repeatedly fitted to resampled data.

500 bootstrap repetitions → record how often each requirement survives → retain requirements selected in ≥ 60% of runs.

2. Estimate their association

The retained requirements are refitted using an unpenalised ordinal model to estimate interpretable odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals.

Selection finds the stable signal; estimation tells us its direction and strength.

How to read the results

An odds ratio (OR) describes how a requirement is associated with being in a higher availability band.

OR = 1

No association

OR > 1

Associated with higher availability

OR < 1

Associated with lower availability

The further from 1, the stronger the association. Confidence intervals show the uncertainty.

But interpretation depends on the type

A score result is about doing a requirement better.

An applicability result describes whether the requirement applies and may indicate a structural difference or gap, rather than an action a facility should take.

Robustness matters more than one model

A requirement is particularly compelling when:

Model B identifies it in the overall programme,

Model A shows that the association remains when facilities are compared within countries,

and Model C shows that it does not disappear when target population is removed.

Agreement across models is the robust core.

A requirement that changes direction or disappears across specifications should be treated more cautiously.

What the analysis can — and cannot — tell us

The analysis can identify requirements that consistently go together with higher or lower vaccine availability, after accounting for important contextual differences.

It can help programmes identify where to investigate, prioritise and improve.

But the data are cross-sectional:

Association is not proof of causation.

A requirement associated with higher availability is not automatically a guaranteed lever that will cause availability to increase if changed.

The results are best viewed as a robust evidence-based ranking of where to look first.
