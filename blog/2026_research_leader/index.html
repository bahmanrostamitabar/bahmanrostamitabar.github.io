This Blog — This is not an argument against leadership. It is an argument for radical honesty about what leadership actually costs — and what it genuinely offers in return.

There is a specific, quiet moment in a research career when the scenery shifts and the air in the room feels different. It usually happens when you realise your morning wasn’t spent at the terminal debugging a complex script or hunched over the microscope, but navigating the labyrinth of budget reconciliations, HR hurdles, and committee agendas. This is the inflection point where the Craftsman — the researcher whose identity is forged in the technical intimacy of discovery — is asked to become the Architect.

This transition is often framed as a natural progression, yet it represents a fundamental mutation in how one contributes to science. While the Craftsman finds a meditative joy in the outliers of a raw data file, the Architect must find meaning in the scaffolding that makes such data possible. This new role offers Agenda Power — the visionary control to define “the next big thing” — but it comes at a cost. Before you step onto that next rung, you must ask if you are prepared for a life where your primary tools are no longer instruments, methods and software, but recruitment, diplomatic influence, and the heavy mantle of institutional strategy.

The Craftsman–Architect Framework

Your Legacy Isn’t the Paper, It’s the People

In the Architect’s world, intellectual impact is measured at scale. You move from the tactile satisfaction of solving a singular, thorny problem to overseeing a vast portfolio of solutions. Your influence is no longer limited by the hours you can spend at the bench; it multiplies through the collective hands of your team.

This shift requires a radical redefinition of output. While the Craftsman obsessively polishes the next publication, the Architect focuses on the professional ecosystem that allows those publications to bloom.

The professional growth of your PhDs and Postdocs becomes your most lasting output — often outsurviving the data itself.

The Bench Gap and the Grief of Lost Discovery

Despite the allure of strategic visibility and the ability to shape priorities, the move upward involves a grieving process that many senior academics feel but few openly admit. This is the Bench Gap — the visceral sense of loss that comes when hands-on research time shrinks to zero.

For the true Craftsman, the work is intrinsically rewarding. There is a specific dopamine hit found in the deep work of coding or the physical engagement of the lab that cannot be replicated by signing off on a grant report. When this technical intimacy is severed, it leaves a void.

What You Lose

🔴 The meditative state of being genuinely absorbed in a technical puzzle for hours without interruption.
🔴 The intimate knowledge of every outlier and anomaly in your own raw data files.
🔴 The singular pride of being the first author — the engine — behind a manuscript.

What You Gain

🟢 The power to define what the lab pursues next at a strategic level.
🟢 The capacity to multiply your intellectual legacy through a dozen careers, not just one CV.
🟢 Institutional influence and the ability to shape your field’s direction.

The Identity Crisis: Am I Still a Scientist?

This leads to the most uncomfortable question in academic leadership: “Am I still a scientist if I don’t do science?” Many research leaders find themselves becoming managers of research rather than practitioners of it.

This evolution creates a state of extreme fragmentation. Instead of the deep focus required to solve a puzzle, the leader’s day is defined by high-frequency context-switching between ten distinct projects, each requiring a different mental register.

ImportantThe Prestige Trap

In the halls of the university, we are taught to conflate prestige with meaning. Leadership roles offer more visibility and larger titles — but if your genuine satisfaction comes from solving hard problems with your own hands, no amount of institutional prestige will compensate for the loss of that craft.

The Hidden Moral Weight

Leadership introduces a level of emotional labor and accountability that a solo researcher rarely faces. When a grant is rejected, it isn’t just your reputation on the line — it is the livelihoods of everyone in your group. You carry the moral weight of their career success, their visas, and their families’ stability.

TipThe Tall Poppy Risk

Success in leadership can trigger unexpected territorial friction. In the competitive garden of academia, there is a recurring tendency to cut down the tall poppy. Navigating these shifting power dynamics requires a political acumen that many researchers find deeply exhausting.

The Full Comparison

To understand the depth of this shift, consider how every fundamental dimension of your working life changes at once.

Table 1 · The Craftsman vs. The Architect — Ten Dimensions

Ownership End-to-End: You own the idea, the data, the analysis, and the draft. Pipeline: You own the funding, the ethics, the talent, and the impact.
Daily Rhythm Deep Work: Immersed in one or two technical problems for hours. High-Frequency Switching: Managing 5–10 distinct project “pulses” daily.
Primary Tool Instruments, software, and specialised methodologies. Budgets, recruitment, and interpersonal influence.
Problem Solving Technical: “Why is this code crashing?” or “Why did the assay fail?” Structural: “Why is this collaboration stalling?” or “How do we pivot after a rejection?”
Control Level Direct: If you want it done right, you do it yourself. Indirect: You achieve results through the hands of others — Delegation.

Role in Output First Author: The primary engine behind the manuscript. Senior / Last Author: The guarantor of the work’s integrity and funding.
Risk Focus Experimental Failure: The hypothesis might be wrong. Sustainability Risk: The lab might run out of money or talent.
Communication Specialist to Specialist: Precise, technical, and narrow. Diplomat / Advocate: Translating “Science” into “Value” for funders and university.
Mental Load Cognitive Depth: Solving a puzzle. Emotional Labour: Managing personalities, egos, and career anxieties.

The Multiplier Effect as a Social Necessity

While the transition is difficult, the value of the research leader to society remains woefully underappreciated. A single brilliant researcher may change a field with one insight, but a brilliant research leader can produce ten such researchers — creating the conditions for an entire movement in science.

This research infrastructure is the bridge between a singular spark of genius and sustained, systemic progress. Both roles are essential, but they are different leverage points in the scientific ecosystem. The question is not which is superior, but which is you.

The Honest Path Forward

The health of our scientific community depends on radical honesty about these roles. The most corrosive element in academia today is what might be called the Legitimacy Problem — leaders who maintain the appearance of being active researchers long after the substance has gone. When those with no hands-on practice continue to dress up administration as research, they make poor decisions and demoralise those still doing the hard, uncertain, slow work of discovery.

The senior academic who admits, “I don’t do bench research anymore — I fund it, shape it, and develop the people who do” preserves the integrity of the entire profession.

As you look at the promotion on the horizon, you must decide which life you want to live. Do you want to stay genuinely hands-on — and are you in an environment that actually values that? Or are you ready to become the Architect, and to find deep meaning in the scaffolding rather than the discovery?

Both paths are honourable. Only one is right for you.

Before you make any career move, it is worth sitting with two separate questions. The first is forward-looking: do I actually want to lead? The second is uncomfortable and present-tense: am I still genuinely a researcher, or have I already drifted?

These checklists won’t make the decision for you. But they will force a level of honesty that most career conversations quietly avoid.

The Harder Question Underneath Both

Many people in academic departments carry the label of researcher while spending the majority of their time on funding, administration, journal work, and strategy. That’s not inherently wrong — those functions matter. But the gap between identity and practice tends to create a quiet dissonance that affects decision-making, self-perception, and how others experience your leadership.

The goal of these checklists isn’t to judge. It’s to help you make a clear-eyed choice — and then commit to it honestly.
