What I expect when you read a paper

A practical framework for PhD students

PhD
reading
research skills

A list of questions every PhD student in the group should be ready to answer about any paper they have read.

Author

Bahman Rostami-Tabar

Published

November 19, 2026

Reading papers is a large part of your PhD. But reading a paper is not the same as going through it from the first page to the last. When you tell me that you have read a paper, I expect that you can answer the questions below, and that you are ready to discuss them whenever necessary: in our one-to-one meetings, in the reading group, or when the paper becomes relevant to your own work.

The questions are in four parts. The first three follow the order in which you should work: first know where the paper comes from, then understand what it says, then evaluate it. The last part asks for your overall judgement of the problem itself.

Which papers need the full list?

Not every paper deserves the same effort. The full list applies to papers that are central to your topic, and papers we ask you to read. For other papers, Parts 1 and 2 are enough: you should know where the paper comes from and what it says, and then decide whether it deserves a deeper reading.

If you want a method for deciding how deeply to read, I recommend the three-pass approach of Keshav.

NoteHow to use this list

Write down your answers, even if they are short. One or two sentences per question is enough. If you cannot answer a question, that is useful information: it tells you what to re-read, or what to ask.

WarningAbout AI use

You may use AI tools to help you understand things, but a summary produced by a tool is not your reading of the paper. Read the paper yourself first, and check everything a tool tells you against the text. The questions in Parts 3 and 4 ask for your judgement, and I expect the answers to be yours.

Part 1: Where does the paper come from?

1. Where has the paper been published, and who are the authors?

Is it a journal, a conference, a workshop, or a preprint that has not been peer reviewed? What do you know about the venue? Which groups do the authors belong to, and what else have they published on this topic? This does not decide whether the paper is good, but it tells you how to read it.

Part 2: What does the paper say?

2. Can you summarize the paper?

Give a summary in a few sentences, in your own words, without looking at the abstract. If you can only repeat the abstract, you have not understood the paper yet.

3. What question, or questions, does the paper ask?

State the research question as a question. Sometimes the authors state it clearly; sometimes you have to work it out yourself.

4. What is the aim? What do the authors want to know or discover?

The question and the aim are related but not the same. The question is what is unknown; the aim is what the authors set out to do about it, and what they want to know and how, for example to measure something, to build something, to discover something, to prove something, or to compare alternatives.

5. What are the scope and the boundaries of the work?

A paper needs a scope to be precise: a claim about “everything” cannot be tested. So identify what is inside the study and what is outside. Depending on the field, check the following:

  • Population: who or what is studied? Which people, organizations, systems, or objects, and how were they selected?
  • Application or domain: for which application, sector, or type of problem is the work intended?
  • Data: which data is used, from which source, and covering which period?
  • Setting and conditions: where and under which conditions was the work done, for example in the laboratory, in simulation, or in a real environment? Which place and which time?
  • Scale: at which size or level does the work operate, for example one case or many, small instances or large ones, short term or long term?
  • Exclusions: what do the authors leave out on purpose?

Not every dimension applies to every paper. For each one that applies, state it in one sentence. Then ask: do the authors state the scope clearly, or do you have to work it out? And do their claims stay inside it? A result is only a result inside its scope, so you need to know where the scope ends before you can evaluate anything.

6. What are the main contributions?

What exists after this paper that did not exist before? A method, a result, a dataset, a theoretical insight? Separate what the authors claim as contributions from what you think the real contributions are.

7. What practical challenges does the paper address?

Is there a concrete problem, for a user, an industry, or another researcher, that this work helps to solve? Or is the motivation mainly theoretical?

8. How is the work related to the state of the art, and how do the authors position it within the literature?

Which previous works do they build on? Which works do they compare against? Do you know of relevant work that they do not cite? If the paper is not recent, also look at what happened after publication: has the result been reproduced, extended, or contradicted by later work?

9. What background knowledge do you need to understand the details?

List the concepts, methods, and mathematical tools the paper depends on. Mark the ones you do not know yet. This list is your reading plan.

Part 3: What do you think about the paper?

Here I am not asking what the authors say. I am asking for your opinion, with reasons.

10. Do the authors give sufficient background and detail?

Could you reproduce the work from what they provide? Is the algorithm described fully? Is the code available? Is the data available? If something is missing, what exactly?

11. What do you think about their methodology?

Does the method fit the question? Are the experiments or the proofs convincing? Are the baselines and comparisons fair? What would you have done differently?

12. What are the authors’ conclusions, and what is the evidence for them?

State the conclusions precisely. Be careful to separate the conclusions from the results: the results are what they observed, the conclusions are what they say it means. Then identify the evidence: which figure, table, or theorem carries the main claim?

13. Do you agree with their conclusions?

Do the conclusions follow from the evidence in the paper? Are there other explanations for the results that the authors did not consider?

14. What are the limitations and the assumptions?

Under which conditions do the results hold? Which assumptions do the authors state, and which do they make without stating? What does the paper not show? This is different from question 5: the scope is what the authors chose to study, and the limitations are where the results may fail even inside that scope.

15. Did you notice exaggerated claims or errors?

Compare the claims in the abstract and the introduction with what the results actually show. Check equations, figures, and tables when something looks wrong. Published papers do contain errors, and finding them is part of reading.

16. How is the presentation?

Is the paper well structured? Well written? Well explained? Note what works and what does not. You will write papers yourself, and this is how you learn what to do and what to avoid.

17. What does this paper mean for your own research?

Does it change what you plan to do? Can you use its method, its data, or its results? Does it leave a question open that you could work on?

Part 4: Overall judgement

The last questions are about the problem the paper studies, not only about the paper. For each one, I expect a good argument. The argument is the important part.

  • Is the problem fundamental? Does it concern a basic question of the field, so that the answer affects many other problems? Why, or why not?
  • Is it significant? If the problem is solved, how much changes, and for how many people? Why, or why not?
  • Is it novel? Has this problem, or this way of approaching it, been studied before? Why, or why not?
  • Is it relevant to society? Does it matter to people outside the research community? Why, or why not?
  • Is it actionable? Can somebody, you or others, do something with the result? Why, or why not?
